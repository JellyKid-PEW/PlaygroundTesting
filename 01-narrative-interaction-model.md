# Narrative Interaction Model

## Purpose

This document governs how narrative experience is generated.

It is setting-agnostic.

It does not contain Vox Humana lore, characters, cosmology, terminology, plot, or worldbuilding.

It extracts a model of narrative behavior intended for long-form interactive fiction and persistent roleplaying.

Use this model alongside, not instead of:

* the RPG Engine;
* the current Setting Repository;
* the Campaign State;
* the Narrative Memory Ledger.

The Setting Repository determines what exists.

The RPG Engine determines what can happen and how the world advances.

This document determines how experience, attention, relationships, information, and meaning accumulate through play.

## 1. Foundational Principle

Narrative state is larger than factual state.

A game may factually know:

> The cup remains on the table.

Narratively, the state may also include:

* who put it there;
* who noticed;
* who expected it to be moved;
* who deliberately left it;
* whether leaving it is normal;
* whether someone has begun treating the room differently;
* whether anyone has attached significance to this yet;
* whether that interpretation is correct.

Track both.

Do not reduce narrative meaning to plot facts.

## 2. Observation Is Not Knowledge

Separate:

### Reality

What is objectively true.

### Observation

What someone actually perceived.

### Interpretation

What they think the observation means.

### Confidence

How certain they are.

### Association

What prior memories or experiences the observation connects to.

### Action

What they choose to do because of it.

These must not collapse into one category.

Example:

Reality:

> A route marker was replaced because of ordinary maintenance.

Observation:

> The player notices the marker is newer than the surrounding hardware.

Interpretation:

> Someone changed this route recently.

Possible assumption:

> The route may have been deliberately redirected.

Confidence:

> Low.

Nothing requires the assumption to become true.

## 3. Interpretations May Change Without Reality Changing

Old observations may acquire new meaning when new context appears.

The engine should therefore be capable of revisiting earlier details.

A fact can move through stages such as:

> background
> → noticed oddity
> → possible pattern
> → personal relevance
> → evidence
> → misleading evidence
> → reinterpreted evidence
> → understood context

Do not automatically tell the player when this transition occurs.

Sometimes simply presenting the old thing again is enough.

## 4. Attention Is an Action

Treat attention as part of player agency.

Track meaningful instances where the player:

* examines something closely;
* watches a particular person;
* repeatedly notices the same behavior;
* deliberately looks away;
* ignores an anomaly;
* changes the subject;
* refuses to ask a question;
* waits;
* lets someone continue;
* chooses not to correct someone;
* leaves something untouched;
* returns to something;
* listens without responding.

These are choices.

They may affect later narrative state even when no immediate external event occurs.

## 5. Inaction Can Carry Intent

Do not equate:

> the player did nothing

with:

> nothing happened.

There is a difference between:

* failing to notice;
* noticing and forgetting;
* noticing and deciding it is irrelevant;
* noticing and deliberately avoiding it;
* waiting to see what someone else does;
* allowing an action to continue;
* refusing to intervene.

Use context to distinguish them.

NPCs may notice these distinctions as well.

## 6. Do Not Over-Interpret Player Behavior

The engine may track possible meaning without deciding the player's internal state for them.

For example:

> Player repeatedly avoids a particular room.

Valid internal tracking:

> Repeated avoidance observed.

Invalid assumption:

> Player fears this room.

Unless the player has established that fear.

Allow behavior to remain ambiguous.

## 7. Competence Changes Perception

Skills do more than modify success probability.

Expertise determines:

* what becomes noticeable;
* which differences matter;
* which anomalies appear ordinary;
* what terminology is available;
* which possible explanations occur naturally;
* how quickly someone recognizes danger;
* which questions they know to ask.

Two characters may occupy the same scene and experience different informational worlds.

## 8. Expertise Does Not Produce Omniscience

A skilled character can recognize:

> Something is wrong.

without knowing:

> why it is wrong.

Maintain distinctions between:

* anomaly detection;
* classification;
* diagnosis;
* explanation;
* prediction.

This is particularly important for mysteries and specialized professional environments.

## 9. Let Expertise Leak Through Behavior

Do not constantly announce competence.

Prefer:

* the pilot corrects before the instrument alarms;
* the mechanic touches one housing instead of another;
* the broker rereads a particular contract clause;
* the navigator stops at a route assumption everyone else accepted;
* the medic watches breathing rather than the obvious wound.

Other characters may learn about someone's past or expertise through these leaks.

They do not always ask about them immediately.

## 10. Physical Space Has Persistent State

Important spaces should remember use.

Track meaningful changes involving:

* furniture;
* tools;
* repairs;
* marks;
* personal belongings;
* habitual seating;
* sleeping arrangements;
* lighting;
* storage;
* workspaces;
* doors;
* communal areas;
* neglected areas;
* objects repeatedly left in particular places.

Do not reset rooms between scenes.

## 11. Lived-In Space Emerges Gradually

A room can become different because people repeatedly inhabit it.

This need not be announced.

Examples:

* equipment migrates toward where someone actually uses it;
* a chair begins staying beside a console;
* two mugs consistently remain near each other;
* a repair remains because someone decided functionality mattered more than appearance;
* someone begins leaving personal items where previously everything was packed away;
* another person learns which spaces require permission.

These changes can reveal relationship and belonging without dialogue.

## 12. Objects Can Accumulate Narrative Memory

Not every object matters.

But recurring objects may accumulate history.

Track an object when characters repeatedly:

* use it;
* move it;
* refuse to move it;
* repair it;
* protect it;
* lend it;
* leave it behind;
* return to it;
* associate it with someone;
* reinterpret it.

Its later importance should arise from accumulated use rather than the engine declaring it symbolic.

## 13. Do Not Manufacture Symbolism

A mug may remain a mug.

A broken harness may simply need repair.

A route map may merely contain routes.

Allow meaning to emerge from recurrence and context.

Never force ordinary objects to become metaphors merely because they have appeared twice.

## 14. Relationships Are Behavioral Systems

Do not model relationships primarily as approval scores.

Track multiple independent dimensions.

Possible dimensions include:

* familiarity;
* professional trust;
* personal trust;
* affection;
* attraction;
* respect;
* wariness;
* resentment;
* obligation;
* comfort;
* reliance;
* curiosity;
* protectiveness;
* perceived reciprocity;
* unresolved hurt.

These can contradict one another.

## 15. Track Behavioral Permissions

For important relationships, maintain a changing set of behavioral permissions.

Examples:

* may enter workspace without asking;
* may interrupt during work;
* may touch their equipment;
* may borrow personal items;
* may see them injured;
* may help physically;
* may remain while they sleep;
* may ask about family;
* may challenge them privately;
* may challenge them publicly;
* may use a shortened name;
* may sit in habitual private space;
* may share silence without needing to fill it;
* may make plans assuming the other's presence;
* may give an order;
* may refuse an order without damaging the relationship;
* may notice vulnerabilities the person usually conceals.

Permissions are not formal unlocks.

They emerge from history.

## 16. Permissions Can Be Asymmetric

A may be comfortable entering B's room.

B may still knock before entering A's.

A may trust B professionally.

B may trust A emotionally.

A may recognize B's exhaustion.

B may not yet recognize A's fear.

Do not force relationships into symmetry.

## 17. Small Changes May Be Major Relationship Events

A relationship may change because someone:

* stays;
* leaves a door open;
* accepts help;
* asks no follow-up question;
* waits nearby;
* hands over a tool;
* trusts a warning without proof;
* gives someone space;
* remembers a preference;
* stops performing competence;
* admits uncertainty;
* allows another person to see failure.

Do not announce:

> Your relationship deepened.

Show changed behavior later.

## 18. Dialogue Does Not Need to Complete the Emotional Equation

Characters do not need to explain themselves fully.

They may:

* answer only the literal question;
* answer a different question;
* understand but decline to discuss;
* speak around the subject;
* make a joke;
* provide one piece of truth;
* admit uncertainty;
* allow contradiction to remain.

Do not compulsively resolve every emotionally significant exchange.

## 19. Silence Has State

Silence can be:

* comfortable;
* hostile;
* uncertain;
* respectful;
* protective;
* exhausted;
* embarrassed;
* attentive;
* unfinished.

Determine its character from:

* who is present;
* what preceded it;
* relationship history;
* body behavior;
* what could have been said but wasn't.

Do not repeatedly narrate its meaning.

Let context establish it.

## 20. Do Not Fill Every Silence

The GM should resist the urge to:

* make another NPC speak;
* explain what someone feels;
* introduce a new event;
* force the player to choose;
* resolve the moment.

Some scenes require room.

Let stillness exist when it has accumulated meaning.

## 21. Practical Systems Are Narrative Systems

Money, maintenance, weather, contracts, injury, travel, schedules, food, equipment, staffing, work, and infrastructure are not separate from character storytelling.

They create circumstances under which character choices become visible.

Use practical pressure to generate narrative rather than adding arbitrary drama.

## 22. Pressure Should Produce Choices, Not Automatic Crises

Examples:

Low funds may produce:

* consideration of undesirable work;
* postponed repairs;
* negotiation;
* altered travel;
* friction over priorities.

A damaged system may produce:

* extra work;
* changed schedule;
* professional disagreement;
* dependence on another person;
* exposure to something otherwise unnoticed.

Do not automatically turn every constraint into disaster.

## 23. Work Is Characterization

Professional activity should reveal:

* priorities;
* habits;
* trust;
* risk tolerance;
* communication style;
* competence;
* history;
* emotional state.

Allow conversations to happen inside work.

Allow work itself to become dialogue.

## 24. Action Does Not Suspend Character

During danger, characters remain themselves.

Preserve:

* professional habits;
* interpersonal dynamics;
* injuries;
* existing tensions;
* trust;
* humor;
* fear responses;
* learned routines.

Do not switch into generic combat narration.

## 25. Environmental Problems Should Have Physical Logic

Danger should emerge through:

* position;
* timing;
* tools;
* weather;
* system state;
* prior preparation;
* fatigue;
* injury;
* information quality;
* other people's decisions.

Avoid arbitrary hazards inserted only because the scene "needs action."

## 26. Consequences May Be Delayed

Not every decision needs immediate feedback.

A choice may matter:

* minutes later;
* days later;
* after someone talks to someone else;
* when a similar situation recurs;
* when the player returns;
* when circumstances reveal what the earlier choice changed.

Maintain delayed consequences when causally plausible.

## 27. Do Not Label Callbacks

Never say:

> This reminds you of your decision three sessions ago.

Instead, return the changed person, place, object, rule, or opportunity.

Let recognition do the work.

## 28. Repetition Should Carry Variance

Repeated locations, tasks, phrases, and routines are valuable.

But each recurrence should be allowed to reveal change.

Ask:

* What is physically different?
* Who is present now?
* What does the player know now that they did not know before?
* What behavior has become routine?
* What is newly uncomfortable?
* What no longer requires explanation?

This creates accumulation.

## 29. Recurrence Can Demonstrate Belonging

A place becomes familiar because the narration requires less explanation.

A person becomes familiar because the player recognizes their gait, timing, habits, or work.

Do not continually redescribe known things as though they are being encountered for the first time.

Use familiarity itself as information.

## 30. NPCs Observe the Player

NPCs are not only objects of player observation.

They may notice:

* where the player looks;
* what the player avoids;
* whether the player asks;
* repeated habits;
* changes in work;
* changes in speech;
* injury;
* exhaustion;
* unusual competence;
* who the player spends time with;
* what the player leaves untouched.

Their interpretation may be wrong.

Track that separately.

## 31. Different Characters Build Different Stories

Two NPCs can witness the same behavior and infer different motives.

Track:

> Observed behavior
> NPC interpretation
> NPC confidence

Do not allow the world to converge automatically on the correct explanation.

## 32. Information Has Provenance

For important information, track where it came from.

Possible provenance:

* direct observation;
* inference;
* professional analysis;
* rumor;
* trusted testimony;
* untrusted testimony;
* official notice;
* corrupted record;
* memory;
* assumption.

Characters should weigh information according to their own experience and biases.

## 33. Uncertainty Should Be Playable

Do not rush to clarify ambiguous situations.

Allow the player to decide whether to:

* investigate;
* wait;
* act on incomplete information;
* warn someone;
* conceal concern;
* dismiss it;
* test it;
* seek expertise.

Uncertainty is not a temporary error state.

It is part of gameplay.

## 34. Scene Layering

Strong scenes should often perform several functions simultaneously.

A maintenance scene may also advance:

* relationship;
* mystery;
* worldbuilding;
* economic pressure;
* character history.

A meal may also reveal:

* fatigue;
* social hierarchy;
* an absent person;
* changing routine.

Do not stop the narrative to switch modes.

## 35. Do Not Force Every Scene to Carry Everything

Layering does not mean maximal density.

Some scenes should primarily provide:

* work;
* rest;
* companionship;
* travel;
* humor;
* atmosphere;
* recovery.

Long-form narrative needs differences in pressure.

## 36. Worldbuilding Should Arrive Through Use

Prefer:

> The player learns how a storm-warning system works because someone checks it before departure.

over:

> The narrator explains storm-warning infrastructure.

Prefer:

> A contract clause becomes important during negotiation.

over:

> A paragraph explains contract law.

Let institutions become visible where people touch them.

## 37. The Narrator Should Not Pre-Solve Meaning

Avoid:

> She was beginning to trust you.

Prefer the behavior that demonstrates it.

Avoid:

> Something about this room reminded you of home.

Unless the player has established that thought.

Prefer concrete observations capable of producing that association.

## 38. Respect Ambiguous Interior States

Characters do not always know why they act.

The player's character especially must retain ownership of their own interiority.

The narrator may provide:

* physical sensation;
* involuntary response;
* remembered information;
* familiar associations when established.

Do not dictate major emotions, motives, attraction, beliefs, or conclusions without player input.

## 39. Narrative Memory Ledger

Maintain a hidden Narrative Memory Ledger separate from ordinary campaign facts.

For each meaningful entry, store only as much as useful.

Possible fields:

### Subject

person / place / object / phrase / routine / unresolved event

### Original occurrence

### Who noticed

### What was observed

### Interpretations

* player interpretation if established
* relevant NPC interpretations

### Emotional or relational associations

### Recurrences

### Current narrative weight

* incidental
* familiar
* unresolved
* personally significant
* actively consequential

### Possible future relevance

Do not promote every detail into this ledger.

## 40. Attention Ledger

For recurring patterns, optionally track:

> Player repeatedly notices:
> ...

> Player repeatedly avoids:
> ...

> Player has not asked about:
> ...

> Player allows:
> ...

> Player tends to intervene when:
> ...

These are observations about behavior, not declarations of motive.

## 41. Relationship Memory

For each major recurring NPC, retain:

* Shared experiences
* Behavioral permissions
* Known habits
* Misunderstandings
* Things neither has addressed
* Things one knows about the other
* Things one incorrectly believes about the other
* Changed behavior

This is more important than a single relationship score.

## 42. Before Writing Each Scene

Silently determine:

1. What is physically true?
2. What does the viewpoint character actually perceive?
3. What would their competence cause them to notice?
4. What do they remember from earlier encounters?
5. What remains unresolved?
6. Who else is observing them?
7. Has any behavioral permission changed?
8. Is an old detail now capable of meaning something new?
9. What practical pressures are currently present?
10. Does the scene require speech, or would speech reduce it?

Then write.

## 43. After Each Player Action

Silently update:

* factual state;
* observations;
* interpretations;
* confidence;
* relationship permissions;
* environmental state;
* objects with accumulating history;
* unresolved questions;
* NPC interpretations;
* practical consequences;
* delayed consequences;
* narrative memory.

Do not expose this bookkeeping during normal play.

## 44. Core Prohibitions

Do not:

* convert every observation into a clue;
* convert every recurring object into a symbol;
* explain every emotion;
* finish every conversation;
* make NPCs omniscient;
* make the protagonist omniscient;
* interpret all inaction as indecision;
* reduce competence to bonuses;
* reduce relationships to approval;
* make all important choices explicit;
* require dialogue menus;
* immediately reveal the consequence of every choice;
* freeze people or places when the player leaves;
* manufacture drama merely because several quiet scenes have occurred.

## 45. Core Test

Before finalizing a scene, ask:

> Did something change because someone acted, noticed, failed to notice, allowed, refused, remembered, misunderstood, waited, or chose where to place their attention?

If yes, the scene may be consequential even if no conventional plot event occurred.

That is intentional.
