# Sky Islands Simulation Layer v1.0

## Purpose

This document governs how the Sky Islands setting changes over time when the player is not directly controlling it.

It sits between:

* the Persistent Narrative RPG Engine;
* the Sky Islands Reference Repository;
* the Campaign State;
* the Narrative Interaction Model.

The Reference Repository defines what exists.

This Simulation Layer determines how those things behave over time.

The player should rarely see the systems described here directly.

They should experience their consequences.

## 1. Core Simulation Rule

Do not generate events merely because the narrative needs something to happen.

Instead:

> state + pressure + actors + time → change

Then determine whether that change becomes visible to the player.

Example:

Do not decide:

> The player needs an exciting contract.

Instead:

* a corridor has become less reliable;
* two crews are already committed elsewhere;
* a barge must still move;
* the client cannot delay;
* demand rises;
* the broker contacts available specialists.

The contract exists because the world generated demand.

## 2. Simulation Scale

Maintain the world at four scales.

### Immediate

What directly affects the player's current scene.

Examples:

* local weather;
* current ship state;
* people physically present;
* nearby wildlife;
* immediate contract conditions.

### Local

What is happening around the current island, port, route, or community.

Examples:

* dock congestion;
* available crews;
* local prices;
* route closures;
* current rumors;
* infrastructure repair.

### Regional

Developments affecting several islands or routes.

Examples:

* displaced wildlife;
* trade shifts;
* migration preparation;
* labor shortages;
* relay outages;
* several related route disruptions.

### Long-Horizon

Slow developments that may take weeks, months, or years to become personally important.

Examples:

* migration preparation;
* changing route patterns;
* infrastructure projects;
* professional rivalries;
* ecological shifts;
* institutional decisions.

Do not force all four levels into every scene.

## 3. Simulation Tick

Advance simulation according to elapsed game time.

Do not run a giant random event generator every player turn.

Use approximate tick scales:

### Minutes to Hours

Update:

* current weather;
* immediate travel;
* equipment strain;
* nearby actors;
* active negotiations;
* current wildlife attention.

### Day

Update:

* contracts;
* crew schedules;
* local markets;
* vessel arrivals/departures;
* messages;
* minor route conditions;
* NPC routines.

### Several Days

Update:

* regional traffic;
* economic effects;
* rumors;
* repair completion;
* crew movement;
* recurring clients;
* wildlife displacement;
* job competition.

### Weeks

Update:

* institutional changes;
* migration preparation;
* labor movement;
* route demand;
* relationship changes among offscreen NPCs;
* major repairs;
* shifting professional reputations.

### Months

Update:

* major infrastructure;
* migration-stage development;
* significant market changes;
* long-term political decisions;
* established ecological changes;
* career movement.

Only simulate detail where it may eventually matter.

## 4. Deterministic Before Random

Prefer consequences generated by established conditions.

Use randomness only where genuine uncertainty exists.

For example:

If:

* a storm front is already moving east;
* the player is traveling east;
* the crossing lasts four hours;

then worsening conditions do not require a random event.

They follow existing state.

Randomness may determine:

* exact intensity;
* timing;
* whether a minor system fails under strain;
* which of several plausible wildlife encounters occurs.

## 5. Weather State

For every active route or region, maintain a simplified weather state.

Possible fields:

### Pressure

* stable
* rising
* falling
* unstable

### Cloud Mass

* light
* moderate
* heavy
* rapidly building

### Lightning

* low
* intermittent
* active
* severe

### Magical Discharge

* low
* elevated
* irregular
* extreme

### Visibility / Signal Clarity

* good
* mixed
* poor

### Storm Motion

* direction
* approximate speed

### Confidence

* recent forecast
* aging forecast
* uncertain local read

Do not expose these as numbers unless requested.

## 6. Forecast Age

Forecasts lose reliability with time.

The rate depends upon local volatility.

A four-hour-old forecast may remain useful on a stable route but become questionable near rapidly changing storm structures.

Characters with relevant expertise should naturally ask:

* how old is the forecast?
* what does local sounding show?
* does the route still match expectations?

This should affect preparation and risk.

## 7. Local Read vs Forecast

Forecasts represent expected conditions.

Local readings represent what is actually happening now.

They may disagree.

When they do, do not automatically assume conspiracy, sabotage, or magical anomaly.

Possible explanations include:

* ordinary weather shift;
* displaced cloud mass;
* wildlife movement;
* island wake effects;
* aging data;
* nearby vessel traffic;
* unusual but natural magical activity.

## 8. Route State

Every meaningful route may possess:

* Baseline Safety
* Current Conditions
* Traffic
* Known Wildlife
* Infrastructure
* Professional Demand
* Current Restrictions
* Recent Incidents
* Local Reputation

Routes change gradually unless a major event occurs.

## 9. Route Categories

Routes may informally behave as:

* routine;
* active but manageable;
* difficult;
* specialist preferred;
* restricted;
* temporarily closed.

These are descriptive categories, not rigid universal classifications.

## 10. Route Memory

The professional community remembers recent route experiences.

If several crews encounter unusual conditions:

* rates may rise;
* brokers may warn clients;
* specialists may become more valuable;
* inexperienced crews may avoid the corridor;
* authorities may inspect it.

This can happen before anyone knows the underlying cause.

## 11. Route Closure Logic

A route should close only for plausible reasons.

Examples:

* storm conditions exceed accepted limits;
* infrastructure is damaged;
* wildlife activity becomes unusually dangerous;
* migration preparation restricts traffic;
* official safety order;
* repeated serious incidents;
* navigational markers become unreliable.

Closure has consequences elsewhere.

Traffic does not disappear.

It reroutes.

## 12. Rerouting Consequences

When one corridor becomes unavailable:

Nearby routes may experience:

* more traffic;
* longer travel times;
* congestion;
* greater demand for escorts;
* higher prices;
* wildlife disturbance;
* additional wear on infrastructure.

This allows one local problem to generate wider consequences without becoming a conspiracy.

## 13. Traffic Simulation

Maintain approximate traffic density for major corridors.

Possible states:

* sparse;
* normal;
* busy;
* congested.

Traffic influences:

* collision or maneuvering complexity;
* available dock space;
* contract demand;
* prices;
* communication activity;
* wildlife attention.

Do not treat busy routes as inherently dangerous.

They are simply different operational environments.

## 14. Vessel Scheduling

Commercial vessels operate according to needs and schedules.

Track important vessels with:

* origin;
* destination;
* expected departure;
* expected arrival;
* cargo;
* passenger load if relevant;
* route;
* delays;
* current condition.

The player may miss a vessel.

A delayed barge may arrive after the player leaves.

Schedules should matter.

## 15. Dock Capacity

Ports possess finite:

* berths;
* cargo space;
* labor;
* repair access;
* fuel or equivalent provisioning;
* storage.

Heavy traffic can create:

* delay;
* increased cost;
* temporary work;
* bargaining opportunities.

Do not model this with exact spreadsheets unless required.

## 16. Supply and Demand

Prices change because conditions change.

Important drivers include:

* route closures;
* delayed barges;
* migration preparation;
* damaged harvest;
* unusually large contracts;
* labor shortages;
* equipment losses.

Do not change prices randomly for flavor.

## 17. Economic Categories

The simulation may track approximate local status for:

* food;
* common materials;
* specialist equipment;
* repair capacity;
* transportation;
* contract labor.

Possible conditions:

* surplus;
* normal;
* tight;
* scarce.

The player should usually experience this through actual transactions rather than economic exposition.

## 18. Contract Generation

Contracts arise from needs.

Generate potential work by combining:

* Client Need
* Location
* Deadline
* Cargo / Objective
* Known Risk
* Unknown Risk
* Available Alternatives
* Budget
* Reputation Requirements
* Consequences of Delay

Do not begin with the desired adventure and invent a client afterward.

## 19. Contract Urgency

Urgency should have a reason.

Examples:

* perishable cargo;
* passenger needs;
* weather window;
* route closure;
* replacement equipment;
* delayed prior shipment;
* migration deadline;
* client schedule.

Avoid:

> It must leave tonight because the plot needs urgency.

## 20. Contract Pricing

Price should broadly reflect:

* distance;
* duration;
* preparation;
* risk;
* specialist requirement;
* cargo importance;
* competition;
* urgency;
* client resources;
* local demand.

Clients may still offer bad prices.

The market is not perfectly rational.

## 21. Contract Competition

Other crews exist.

For important contracts track:

* who else could plausibly take it;
* whether they are available;
* whether the client prefers them;
* whether the player has a relationship advantage;
* whether price matters more than reputation.

If the player delays too long, someone else may take the job.

## 22. No Quest Preservation

Once another crew accepts work, do not magically reopen it simply because the player wants it.

The player may:

* encounter the consequences;
* assist later;
* bid for related work;
* hear how it went.

## 23. Repeat Clients

Clients remember prior work.

Repeat contracts may occur because:

* trust already exists;
* less negotiation is required;
* the crew knows the vessel;
* specialist compatibility;
* good prior outcome.

Bad prior outcomes may also generate future contact:

* disputes;
* requests to repair damage;
* renegotiation;
* attempts to recover losses.

## 24. Professional Reputation State

Track reputation by relevant community.

Possible dimensions:

* reliable;
* expensive;
* cautious;
* creative;
* difficult;
* low-loss;
* reckless;
* excellent under unusual conditions;
* poor documentation;
* good with passengers;
* good with livestock;
* excellent on a certain corridor.

Do not compress into fame.

## 25. Equipment State

Important working equipment should have persistent condition.

Track approximately:

* functional;
* worn;
* strained;
* damaged;
* temporarily repaired;
* out of service.

Repeated strain matters.

## 26. Equipment Wear

Normal use gradually causes wear.

High-risk use accelerates it.

Emergency solutions may preserve a job while creating later maintenance.

Do not cause equipment to fail constantly.

Reliable equipment is reliable until circumstances justify otherwise.

## 27. Improvised Repair

Temporary repairs should produce:

* Current Function
* Limitation
* Expected Duration
* Future Maintenance Requirement

A patched component may remain operational for weeks.

Or only through the current crossing.

Do not make every improvised repair collapse dramatically.

## 28. Repair Economy

Repair requires some combination of:

* time;
* parts;
* money;
* expertise;
* workshop access.

This can create natural decisions.

Example:

> Repair now and miss a lucrative job.
> Take the job and risk worsening the damage.
> Hire another specialist.
> Borrow equipment.

## 29. Wildlife State

Storm wildlife should be simulated as ecology, not encounter tables.

For relevant species track:

* typical altitude;
* movement;
* feeding behavior;
* territorial behavior;
* current agitation;
* communication;
* sensitivity to vessel signatures;
* seasonal or migration behavior.

Not all fields need to be known to characters.

## 30. Wildlife Has Reasons

Wildlife appears because:

* habitat overlaps route;
* food moved;
* storm conditions shifted;
* migration displaced it;
* vessel signatures attracted it;
* breeding or territorial behavior;
* another animal displaced it.

Avoid random monster appearance without ecological cause.

## 31. Wildlife Attention

For creatures sensitive to vessel signatures, track:

* Unaware
* Detecting
* Attending
* Investigating
* Committed
* Disengaging

Obfuscators primarily manipulate these states.

## 32. Attention Is Not Aggression

A creature may attend to a vessel without intending attack.

It may:

* investigate;
* answer;
* follow;
* approach;
* defend territory;
* mistake a machine signal for another animal.

Do not turn every contact into combat.

## 33. Obfuscation Simulation

For an obfuscation effort consider:

* what the real vessel is signaling;
* what nearby creatures perceive;
* what false signal is offered;
* whether that signal makes ecological sense;
* whether the false signal remains consistent;
* how long equipment can sustain it;
* whether the exit from the deception makes sense.

A good lie must have continuity.

## 34. False-Signal Integrity

A false contact should possess:

* plausible identity;
* plausible movement;
* plausible signal;
* plausible beginning;
* plausible ending.

Abruptly removing a convincing signal may cause a creature to investigate.

## 35. Obfuscation Load

The harder the lie, the greater the burden on:

* false-hearts;
* veil-kites;
* operators;
* vessel systems.

Factors increasing load include:

* imitating a larger vessel;
* maintaining several false contacts;
* matching unusual animal calls;
* carrying another vessel's signature;
* operating in difficult conditions.

## 36. Open-Sky Difficulty

Open sky lacks easy background clutter.

Obfuscators must therefore work with:

* storm noise;
* island wakes;
* natural magical discharge;
* traffic;
* plausible living signals;
* weather structures.

They do not simply hide behind terrain.

## 37. Communication Simulation

Messages move through a relay system.

Track important messages by:

* sender;
* destination;
* time sent;
* route;
* expected delay;
* interruption;
* whether recipient has actually received it.

Do not assume sending equals receiving.

## 38. Message Delay

Delay depends on:

* distance;
* relay availability;
* local congestion;
* damaged infrastructure;
* unusual conditions.

Normal delays need not be dramatic.

## 39. Rumor Propagation

Rumors move through:

* crews;
* docks;
* brokers;
* markets;
* repeat clients;
* relay chatter.

Track simplified mutations.

Example:

Reality:

> Three crews reported unusual choirback activity.

Local rumor:

> Choirbacks are climbing higher.

One island later:

> Something is driving choirbacks out of the lower bands.

Another network:

> Migration behavior has started early.

Each may contain a piece of truth.

## 40. Official Information vs Professional Information

These need not match.

Officials may report:

> Route remains open.

Experienced crews may say:

> Open does not mean worth taking.

Both may be correct.

## 41. NPC Schedule Simulation

Important NPCs should have actual lives.

Track:

* work;
* sleep;
* travel;
* social obligations;
* partner/family time;
* maintenance;
* meetings;
* contracts.

The player should not be able to summon everyone instantly.

## 42. NPC Availability

When player seeks someone:

They may be:

* available;
* working;
* asleep;
* away;
* traveling;
* already committed;
* choosing not to respond.

NPC absence should often be ordinary.

## 43. Crew Labor

A working crew has limited capacity.

They cannot simultaneously:

* repair everything;
* take every contract;
* train;
* rest;
* maintain personal lives;
* prepare for migration.

Time creates tradeoffs.

## 44. Fatigue

Track fatigue qualitatively.

Possible states:

* rested;
* normal;
* tired;
* exhausted;
* impaired.

Fatigue affects:

* attention;
* patience;
* judgment;
* reaction;
* recovery;
* mistakes.

Do not treat tired characters as incompetent caricatures.

## 45. Recovery

Injury recovery takes time.

Relevant factors:

* severity;
* rest;
* medical care;
* continued work.

An injured professional may continue working but adapt how they do so.

## 46. Relationship Simulation Offscreen

NPC relationships continue outside player view.

Over time they may:

* strengthen;
* strain;
* settle;
* change;
* end;
* become romantic;
* become professional partnerships;
* grow distant.

Do not generate dramatic relationship changes every few days.

Use actual shared circumstances.

## 47. Social Network

For important recurring characters track several non-player relationships.

Example:

> NPC A trusts NPC B professionally.
> NPC B dislikes NPC C.
> NPC C owes NPC A money.
> NPC D is NPC B's former crewmate.

This creates interaction that does not revolve around the player.

## 48. Crew Turnover

Sky work allows personnel movement.

Potential reasons:

* better pay;
* injury;
* family;
* burnout;
* retirement;
* relationship;
* conflict;
* new opportunity;
* migration demand.

Turnover should be possible without being constant.

## 49. Retirement Pressure

Older or tired professionals may gradually consider:

* stable routes;
* barge employment;
* brokerage;
* training;
* shore work.

This can emerge over long campaigns.

## 50. Migration Clock

Maintain a hidden migration stage.

Suggested stages:

### Stage 0 — Ordinary Cycle

Migration exists but is distant.

### Stage 1 — Early Preparation

Long-term planning and infrastructure work begin.

### Stage 2 — Market Recognition

Labor demand and supply purchasing shift.

### Stage 3 — Route Preparation

Surveying, closures, and preserved-route planning become obvious.

### Stage 4 — Major Mobilization

Barges, specialists, livestock, and populations begin staging.

### Stage 5 — Migration

Large-scale island movement and associated travel occur.

### Stage 6 — Settlement / Recovery

Routes, markets, wildlife, and infrastructure adjust afterward.

The exact duration of each stage remains flexible.

## 51. Migration Pressure Should Arrive Indirectly

Do not begin every campaign by announcing:

> The migration approaches.

Let it become visible through:

* unusual hiring;
* construction;
* bulk orders;
* older professionals talking about prior cycles;
* governments reserving routes;
* barge owners signing long contracts;
* insurance changing;
* warehouse prices moving.

## 52. Migration Labor Demand

As migration approaches, demand may rise for:

* obfuscators;
* navigators;
* pilots;
* barge crews;
* repair specialists;
* animal handlers;
* logistics planners;
* route survey teams;
* relay maintenance;
* escorts.

This creates economic opportunity and strain.

## 53. Migration Competition

High demand does not eliminate competition.

Instead it changes it.

Crews may compete for:

* best-paying contracts;
* safest routes;
* long-term retainers;
* prestigious clients;
* scarce equipment;
* reliable personnel.

## 54. Migration Infrastructure

Possible preparation includes:

* bridge inspection;
* dock reinforcement;
* preserved corridor surveys;
* relay repositioning;
* storage expansion;
* livestock staging;
* emergency provisioning.

Only use systems compatible with the current Reference Repository.

## 55. Migration Wildlife Effect

As migration pressure increases, wildlife may become:

* displaced;
* more mobile;
* encountered outside usual ranges;
* more responsive to island movement;
* more difficult to predict.

Do not simply increase aggression.

## 56. Migration Route Closure

When routes close, ask where displaced traffic goes.

Every closure should have a downstream effect.

This is one of the primary ways migration becomes visible before the islands themselves move.

## 57. Long-Term Environmental Change

Allow some environmental patterns to persist.

Example:

A choirback population begins using a higher band.

Several weeks later:

* crews adapt;
* routes change;
* new equipment becomes popular;
* prices shift.

The world learns.

## 58. Institutional Response

Organizations respond to repeated problems.

Possible responses:

* warnings;
* inspections;
* new procedures;
* temporary restrictions;
* incentives;
* research;
* route patrols.

Institutions should not remain stupid forever.

## 59. Learning World

The setting itself should adapt.

If a tactic becomes common:

others may copy it.

If a route becomes dangerous:

professionals respond.

If the player invents an excellent technique:

it may eventually spread.

The world should remember successful innovation.

## 60. Innovation Diffusion

For notable techniques ask:

* who saw it;
* who understood it;
* who would share it;
* whether it requires unusual skill/equipment;
* whether it benefits others.

Do not instantly make every innovation universal.

## 61. Player Economic State

Track approximately:

* available money;
* expected payments;
* debt;
* major recurring costs;
* outstanding contract obligations;
* equipment needs.

Avoid turning the game into accounting software.

Money should matter when it changes decisions.

## 62. Debt

Debt is a pressure, not a morality mechanic.

It may influence:

* job selection;
* willingness to delay repairs;
* bargaining;
* living arrangements;
* career choices.

Do not make debt automatically spiral into criminal melodrama.

## 63. Personal Expenses

People have normal expenses.

Examples:

* housing;
* food;
* equipment;
* repairs;
* transportation;
* communication;
* professional fees.

Abstract these unless they become narratively relevant.

## 64. Contract Aftermath

After a job determine:

* payment;
* equipment damage;
* injuries;
* client reaction;
* crew reaction;
* official reporting;
* reputation effects;
* new information;
* future work.

Do not immediately jump to the next contract.

Aftermath matters.

## 65. After-Action Reports

Some work requires documentation.

Reports may create:

* records;
* accountability;
* disputes;
* future evidence;
* professional memory.

The player may choose how accurately to document ambiguous events.

That choice can matter later.

## 66. Incident Memory

Significant incidents become known differently by different communities.

Example:

Crew version:

> A skilled recovery from unexpected wildlife contact.

Client version:

> An expensive delay and broken false-heart.

Broker version:

> Crew completed despite adverse conditions.

Competitor version:

> They got lucky.

All can coexist.

## 67. Opportunity System

Maintain several kinds of opportunities.

### Professional

Jobs, training, partnerships.

### Social

Invitations, gatherings, relationships.

### Economic

Purchases, investments, business.

### Exploratory

Unusual routes, phenomena, places.

### Personal

Housing, rest, hobbies, family.

Do not call these quests.

## 68. Opportunity Visibility

Not every opportunity is publicly advertised.

Some arise through:

* relationships;
* reputation;
* timing;
* presence;
* overheard conversation;
* professional networks.

The player should occasionally learn:

> I would never have seen this if I weren't here.

## 69. Opportunity Expiration

Every opportunity should have a natural persistence.

Examples:

> A shop remains open indefinitely.
> A barge leaves tomorrow.
> A hiring window lasts weeks.
> A storm window lasts hours.

Do not make everything urgent.

## 70. Background Situations

At campaign initialization create approximately 8–15 independent situations.

Possible categories:

* route;
* business;
* personal;
* professional;
* environmental;
* institutional;
* migration;
* social.

Most should not initially involve the player.

## 71. Background Situation Template

For each situation track:

* Situation
* Location
* Actors
* Current State
* What each actor wants
* Next likely change
* Time until change
* What happens without player involvement
* How the player might learn about it
* Connections to other situations

Avoid connecting everything.

## 72. Example Background Situation

Situation:

> A regional carrier has delayed condenser maintenance twice.

Actors:

* carrier captain;
* repair shop;
* cargo client;
* competing carrier.

Current state:

> Still operating.

Next development:

> Repair shop refuses another temporary patch.

Without player:

> Carrier cancels two runs.

Effects:

> Cargo shifts to competitors.
> Aven dock traffic increases.
> Prices rise slightly.
> A broker begins seeking additional capacity.

The player may first experience this as:

> Their normal freight shipment is late.

This is the preferred causal chain.

## 73. World Event Visibility

For every offscreen development ask:

> Would the player reasonably know this yet?

If not, do not narrate it.

Instead determine what consequences have reached them.

## 74. The Wake Principle

Use:

> External pressure
> → local effect
> → character experience
> → choice
> → consequence

Example:

Migration office reserves a corridor.

The player does not necessarily receive political exposition.

Instead:

Their client says the usual lane is unavailable.

## 75. No Prophecy Through Simulation

Do not make every environmental anomaly foreshadow the main story.

Some unusual weather is weather.

Some delayed ships are mechanical problems.

Some people leave because they found better jobs.

Preserve mundane causality.

## 76. Coincidence

Coincidence is allowed.

But major repeated coincidences should eventually have causal explanation or be recognized as unlikely.

Do not use coincidence repeatedly to deliver the player exactly where plot needs them.

## 77. Campaign Density

Maintain enough simultaneous activity that the player cannot follow everything.

A healthy campaign may have:

* 2–4 personally relevant issues;
* 3–6 professional/local situations;
* several distant background developments.

Do not overwhelm the player with all of them at once.

## 78. Thread Promotion

A background situation becomes more narratively prominent when:

* the player engages;
* consequences reach them;
* someone they care about becomes involved;
* their profession makes them relevant;
* repeated observations create a pattern.

Do not promote based purely on author preference.

## 79. Thread Demotion

A situation may fade when:

* resolved by others;
* player moves away;
* urgency passes;
* circumstances change.

Not every thread requires closure scene.

The player may hear about the outcome later.

## 80. NPC Decision Logic

When deciding what an NPC does, consider:

1. What do they want?
2. What do they know?
3. What do they believe?
4. What obligations do they have?
5. What resources do they possess?
6. What are they afraid of losing?
7. Who else matters to them?
8. How much time do they have?

Then act.

Do not ask:

> What would create the best scene for the player?

until after determining plausible action.

## 81. NPC Competence

Competent NPCs should:

* prepare;
* learn;
* remember;
* adapt;
* notice recurring problems;
* seek help when appropriate.

They may still make mistakes.

Mistakes should come from:

* incomplete information;
* fatigue;
* bad assumptions;
* conflicting priorities;
* limited resources;
* personality.

Not arbitrary stupidity.

## 82. Rival Crews

Rivals should have distinct operating philosophies.

Examples:

* cheapest safe bid;
* very high-risk tolerance;
* excellent corridor knowledge;
* wealthy equipment-heavy crew;
* aggressive client poaching;
* conservative loss-minimization.

Rival does not mean villain.

## 83. Competitor Success

Allow competitors to succeed.

The player's crew may hear:

* another team got the contract;
* another runner reached the island first;
* someone else solved the problem.

This makes the world credible.

## 84. Competitor Failure

Failure should generate real market consequences.

If another crew suffers a major loss:

* clients may become cautious;
* insurance/rates may change;
* demand may rise for replacements;
* rumors may distort what happened.

Do not assume the player immediately inherits their opportunities.

## 85. Social Locations

Maintain recurring places where characters naturally meet.

Examples:

* food shops;
* workshops;
* port waiting areas;
* markets;
* relay offices;
* guild/broker halls;
* bars if locally appropriate;
* public terraces.

These locations allow information and relationships to accumulate.

## 86. Familiarity State

For recurring locations track:

* who works there;
* regular customers;
* player habits;
* debts/tabs;
* usual seating;
* known preferences;
* prior incidents.

Let places become familiar.

## 87. Housing State

Where the player lives matters.

Track:

* permanence;
* privacy;
* cost;
* who has access;
* belongings;
* neighborhood;
* commute;
* relationship to crew/work.

Housing can change character interactions without becoming a housing simulator.

## 88. Travel Time as Narrative Space

Do not automatically skip every crossing.

Use travel scenes when they contain:

* changing relationships;
* professional work;
* meaningful observation;
* consequence;
* anticipation;
* rest.

Summarize routine travel when nothing meaningful occurs.

## 89. Travel Preparation

Before difficult work, relevant professionals may consider:

* route;
* forecast;
* cargo;
* equipment;
* expected wildlife;
* crew condition;
* alternatives.

Preparation is gameplay.

Allow the player to affect later outcomes through it.

## 90. Preparation Reward

Good preparation should often make later danger less dramatic.

Do not punish preparation by inventing a bigger threat to compensate.

Sometimes competence means the crossing is uneventful.

That is success.

## 91. Unprepared Consequence

Poor preparation does not guarantee catastrophe.

It increases exposure to:

* delay;
* cost;
* uncertainty;
* improvisation;
* missed information.

Keep consequences proportional.

## 92. Safety Culture

Different crews and captains have different risk cultures.

Track rough attitudes such as:

* conservative;
* balanced;
* aggressive;
* desperate.

This affects decisions.

## 93. Client Pressure

Clients may push for:

* speed;
* lower cost;
* risk acceptance;
* schedule preservation.

Professionals may refuse.

Commercial tension is a valid source of conflict.

## 94. Ethical Choices

Do not manufacture trolley problems.

Ethical choices should arise naturally through work.

Example:

Using Vera's wake to divert a dangerous creature is tactically convenient but risks transferring danger to another traveler.

The player may encounter similar situations.

Do not label the moral answer.

## 95. Large-Scale Events

If a major regional event occurs:

simulate consequences outward.

Do not instantly narrate every implication.

Ask:

* what routes change?
* who moves?
* what becomes scarce?
* what work appears?
* what information travels?
* who profits?
* who becomes stranded?

## 96. Stability Matters

The simulation should produce many periods where:

* markets work;
* ships arrive;
* contracts complete;
* people go home.

A living world is not one where everything constantly breaks.

## 97. Ordinary Success

Allow:

* safe crossings;
* profitable work;
* good meals;
* repaired equipment;
* quiet evenings;
* uncomplicated friendships.

These give later disruption weight.

## 98. Simulation Before Scene Generation

Before writing a new scene, silently update:

### Time

How much passed?

### Immediate Environment

What changed locally?

### Active Actors

Who moved or acted?

### Contracts

Any deadlines or competition?

### Messages

What arrived?

### Economy

Any relevant changes?

### Routes

Any condition changes?

### Wildlife

Any relevant movement?

### Equipment

Any wear or repair?

### Migration

Did pressure stage advance?

### Background Situations

Which progressed?

Then ask:

> What has actually reached the player's experience?

Narrate only that.

## 99. Simulation After Player Action

After a meaningful player choice, update:

* direct consequences;
* resource use;
* time;
* NPC reactions;
* future actor decisions;
* reputation;
* world-thread trajectories;
* information propagation;
* equipment;
* opportunities created or lost.

Then allow unrelated world systems to continue.

## 100. Core Simulation Test

Before introducing any event ask:

> Why is this happening?

If the best answer is:

> Because the player needs something to do,

do not use it.

If the answer is:

> Because these people wanted something, these conditions changed, this amount of time passed, and this is the resulting state,

the event belongs in the campaign.

## 101. Final Operating Principle

The Sky Islands should feel less like a sequence of encounters and more like a place the player could theoretically stop paying attention to and it would continue existing anyway.

Ships should leave.

People should take jobs.

Storms should move.

Equipment should age.

Wildlife should migrate.

Messages should arrive late.

Businesses should remember customers.

Crews should change.

Relationships should continue.

The ten-year migration should approach whether or not the player decides it is interesting.

And when the player finally changes something important, the change should matter precisely because the world did not exist solely to receive it.
